§ 01 001 / 007
Community consensus

What experienced buyers already know.

Before you spend a dollar, here are the patterns people learn the hard way — pulled from years of user reports, independent lab testing, and expensive mistakes. Consider this the baseline. Read it, then read the rest.

† Patterns drawn from public user reports, independent third-party test results, and peer-shared outcomes over multiple years.

‡ Nothing below is an endorsement of any specific vendor. We're describing what to look for, not who to buy from.

Item 01 · Longevity signals safety ↳ pattern

Vendors with a 15+ year track record rarely fake their lab reports.

A business built over a decade has too much to lose from one bad batch. The vendors cutting corners are usually the new brands with a slick site and no real history behind them.

Item 02 · Consensus > recommendation ↳ pattern

Hundreds of ordinary reviews beat one loud influencer pick.

A single glowing endorsement is marketing. Hundreds of quiet, independent reports over several years is actual evidence. Weight your trust in that direction.

Item 03 · Where vials really come from

Most online vendors source from China.

US-based resellers buy overseas, mark it up, and ship it to you. That's the norm — not the exception. The convenience of a domestic vendor can cost up to ten times the overseas price for the same product.

Item 04 · Price tells you something

Don't chase the cheapest listing.

Prices well below the going rate usually mean one of two things: the product is fake, or the vendor won't ship at all. If a price looks too good to be true, assume it is.

§ 02 003 / 007
Evaluation criteria

How to evaluate any
peptide vendor.

Here's a simple checklist to run any vendor through. If they can't meet these basics, move on. You're not looking for perfect — you're just ruling out the obvious risks before anything goes into your body.

Scroll to read 9 checks
01 Non-negotiable

A COA for your specific batch

A COA (Certificate of Analysis) is a lab report that says what's actually in the vial. It has to match the exact batch you received — a generic one from another lot doesn't count.

02 Chemistry

99%+ purity, tested properly

The COA should show at least 99% purity, confirmed by HPLC and mass spectrometry — the two standard lab methods for measuring what's really in the vial. Anything vaguer than a number is not proof.

03 Disclosure

Clear product information

You should see a real label, the dose per vial, and instructions for mixing it (reconstitution). Marketing copy about how amazing the product is doesn't count as information.

04 Payment

Real payment options

Credit card is better than crypto-only. If a package never shows up or a vial looks off, a chargeback is often your only way to get your money back.

05 Support

Someone who answers

Email them a specific question before you order — something like "which batch am I getting?" If nobody replies within a couple of days, they won't be there when something actually goes wrong either.

06 Logistics

Quick US shipping — cold when needed

Peptides break down when they sit in transit too long. Some also need refrigeration end-to-end (cold-chain shipping). This is basic chemistry, not a nice-to-have.

07 Reputation

A reputation you can check

Look for consistent positive feedback across several independent places, not a wall of five-star testimonials on the vendor's own website. Anyone can write their own reviews.

08 Verification

A COA you can verify

Some independent labs (Janoshik is one) let you type a certificate ID into their site to confirm it's real. If a COA can't be checked at the source, treat it as a picture — not proof.

09 Rule zero

Order · Test · Trust

Don't take any vendor's word for it. Order a small amount, get it independently tested if you can, and only then start trusting the batch. Repeat for every new lot.

§ 03 004 / 007
Instant disqualifiers

Red flags you can spot in 30 seconds.

Open the vendor's website and scan for these. If you see any of them, close the tab and try another vendor. Your instincts are the first line of defense — trust them.

  1. × 01

    Typos and a messy website

    If they can't be bothered to proofread the homepage, they're almost certainly not being careful with the peptides.

    craft
  2. × 02

    Only stock photos

    No real photos of the lab, the team, or the facility — just generic beakers and models in lab coats. If they can't show you who they are, ask yourself why.

    identity
  3. × 03

    Crypto is the only way to pay

    Once you send crypto, it's gone. There's no bank to call, no chargeback, no way to reverse it. Convenient for a scammer, risky for you.

    payment
  4. × 04

    Prices way under everyone else

    Either the product is fake, the vendor plans to disappear with your money, or you're paying for something that isn't what the label says. There's no fourth option.

    pricing
  5. × 05

    No COAs — or a generic one with no date

    If a COA has no batch number or date on it, it's basically decoration — not proof of anything. A real certificate points to a real batch, tested on a specific day.

    documentation
  6. × 06

    Big claims, no receipts

    Words like "miracle," "revolutionary," or "clinically proven" with no studies attached. As a rule, the louder the claim, the thinner the evidence behind it.

    rhetoric
  7. × 07

    Great on forums, empty on their own site

    Fake forum praise is cheap and easy to produce. If a vendor looks beloved online but has no substance on their actual website — no address, no team, no batch info — it's usually bait.

    signals
